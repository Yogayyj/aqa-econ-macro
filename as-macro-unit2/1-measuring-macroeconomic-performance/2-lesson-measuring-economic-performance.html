2. Why Do Economists Use Macroeconomic Indicators?

Definition:
Macroeconomic indicators are data used to measure the performance of an economy.

Governments, central banks, firms and economists use macroeconomic indicators to judge whether an economy is performing well.

These indicators help answer questions such as:

  • Is the economy growing?
  • Are prices stable?
  • Is unemployment low?
  • Is the country competitive internationally?
  • Are income and living standards improving?

3. Measuring Economic Growth

3.1 Gross Domestic Product, GDP

Definition:
GDP is the total value of goods and services produced in an economy over a period of time.

GDP is one of the most common measures of economic activity. If GDP increases, it suggests that the economy is producing more output.

Example:
If a country produces more cars, food, financial services and education services than before, its GDP is likely to increase.

3.2 Nominal GDP

Definition:
Nominal GDP is GDP measured using current prices, without adjusting for inflation.

Nominal GDP can rise because output has increased, but it can also rise simply because prices have increased.

Nominal GDP may exaggerate economic growth if inflation is high.

3.3 Real GDP

Definition:
Real GDP is GDP adjusted for inflation.

Real GDP is more useful than nominal GDP when measuring economic growth because it shows whether actual output has increased.

Real GDP = Nominal GDP / Price index × 100
Example:
If nominal GDP increases by 8% but inflation is 5%, real GDP has increased by approximately 3%.

3.4 Real GDP per Capita

Definition:
Real GDP per capita is real GDP divided by the population.
Real GDP per capita = Real GDP / Population

Real GDP per capita is often used as a measure of average living standards.

However, it is only an average. It does not show how income is distributed.

4. Measuring Price Stability

4.1 Consumer Price Index, CPI

Definition:
The Consumer Price Index measures changes in the average price of a basket of goods and services bought by households.

CPI is used to calculate inflation. A base year is given an index value of 100.

Example:
If CPI rises from 100 to 110, the average price level has increased by 10% compared with the base year.

4.2 Inflation Rate

Definition:
Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level.
Inflation rate = Current CPI - Previous CPI / Previous CPI × 100
Example:
If CPI rises from 120 to 126, inflation is:

(126 - 120) / 120 × 100 = 5%
CPI may not perfectly measure every household’s cost of living because different households buy different goods and services.

5. Measuring Unemployment

5.1 Unemployment

Definition:
Unemployment occurs when people who are willing and able to work cannot find a job.

Unemployment is an important indicator of economic performance because it shows whether labour resources are being fully used.

5.2 ILO Measure of Unemployment

Definition:
The ILO measure counts people as unemployed if they are without work, available to start work and actively seeking employment.
Unemployment rate = Number unemployed / Labour force × 100
The unemployment rate may not show underemployment, where people work fewer hours than they would like or are overqualified for their jobs.

6. Measuring Productivity

6.1 Productivity

Definition:
Productivity measures output per unit of input.

The most common measure is labour productivity.

Labour productivity = Output / Number of workers
Example:
If 10 workers produce 1,000 units, labour productivity is 100 units per worker.
Higher productivity can increase long-run economic growth, reduce costs and improve international competitiveness.

7. Measuring the Current Account

7.1 Balance of Payments on Current Account

Definition:
The current account records a country’s trade in goods, trade in services, primary income and secondary income with the rest of the world.

A current account deficit means more money is flowing out than flowing in through current account transactions.

A current account surplus means more money is flowing in than flowing out.

Example:
If a country imports more goods and services than it exports, this may contribute to a current account deficit.
A current account deficit is not always bad. It may reflect strong domestic demand or imports of capital goods that support future growth.

8. Measuring Inequality

8.1 Gini Coefficient

Definition:
The Gini coefficient is a measure of income inequality within an economy.

A lower Gini coefficient indicates a more equal distribution of income. A higher Gini coefficient indicates a more unequal distribution of income.

Real GDP per capita may rise, but if inequality also rises, many people may not experience higher living standards.

10. Exam-style Questions

Quick Check

  • Explain the difference between nominal GDP and real GDP.
  • Why is real GDP per capita useful when comparing living standards?
  • Why might CPI not accurately measure the cost of living for every household?
  • Explain one limitation of using the unemployment rate as a measure of economic performance.
  • Why can a current account deficit be a concern for a government?
  • Explain why a rise in real GDP may not improve living standards for everyone.

Lesson Summary

  • Macroeconomic indicators help measure how well an economy is performing.
  • Real GDP measures economic growth after adjusting for inflation.
  • Real GDP per capita gives an average measure of living standards.
  • CPI is used to calculate inflation and assess price stability.
  • Unemployment measures show how fully labour resources are used.
  • Productivity is important for long-run growth and competitiveness.
  • The current account measures external economic performance.
  • The Gini coefficient helps measure income inequality.
  • No single indicator gives a complete picture of economic performance.
