2. What are Index Numbers?

Definition:
An index number is a statistic, with a base value of 100, used to measure changes in a selection of related variables.

Index numbers are used to measure changes over time. Economists often use index numbers because they make large or complicated data easier to compare.

The starting point for an index is called the base year. The base year is given a value of 100.

Example:
If a price index rises from 100 to 120, this means the price has increased by 20% compared with the base year.

3. Key Terms

Base year: the starting point for an index, where the value is 100.
Consumer Price Index, CPI: a measure of the price level and inflation.
Basket of goods: the goods and services included in a price index.
Weight: a way to reflect the relative importance of each item in an index.

4. CPI, Basket of Goods and Weights

4.1 Consumer Price Index

The Consumer Price Index measures changes in the average price of a weighted basket of consumer goods and services purchased by households.

CPI is the most commonly used measure of inflation.

4.2 Basket of Goods

To construct the CPI, economists first decide which goods and services should be included in the basket. This is often done by asking households to record their spending over a period of time.

Example:
Many years ago, CDs may have been included in a typical basket of goods. Today, music downloads or streaming services are more likely to be included instead.

4.3 Weights

Weights are used because households spend more on some goods than others. The greater the percentage of total spending on an item, the larger the weight attached to that item.

Example:
If a typical household spends 12.3% of its income on an item, and the total weights are set at 1000, the item will be given a weight of 123.
The use of weights means that a given percentage increase in the price of an important item has a greater effect on the overall price index.

5. Calculating a New Price Index for One Item

If the price of one item changes, its new price index can be calculated using the formula below.

New price index = New price / Original price × 100

Clicker Example: Chicken Price Index

Base year price of chicken = $3.00
Current year price of chicken = $3.60

Step 1: Write the formula
New price index = New price ÷ Original price × 100
Step 2: Substitute the numbers
New price index = 3.60 ÷ 3.00 × 100
Step 3: Calculate
3.60 ÷ 3.00 × 100 = 120
Step 4: Interpret the answer
The price index is 120, so the price of chicken increased by 20% compared with the base year.

This means the price of chicken has increased by 20% compared with the base year.

6. Constructing a Weighted Price Index

A weighted price index takes account of the relative importance of different items in the basket of goods.

Weighted price index = Sum of index × weight / Sum of weights
Item Price in Year 1 Index in Year 1 Price in Year 2 Index in Year 2 Weight Index in Year 2 × Weight
Coffee $2.00 100 $2.20 110 30 3300
Milk $0.80 100 $1.00 125 20 2500
Sugar $1.20 100 $0.96 80 10 800
Tea $1.50 100 $1.95 130 40 5200
Total 100 11800
Overall price index 118

Clicker Example: Weighted Price Index

Use the final column of the table to calculate the overall weighted price index.

Step 1: Multiply each item index by its weight
Coffee: 110 × 30 = 3300
Milk: 125 × 20 = 2500
Sugar: 80 × 10 = 800
Tea: 130 × 40 = 5200
Step 2: Add the weighted values
3300 + 2500 + 800 + 5200 = 11800
Step 3: Add the weights
30 + 20 + 10 + 40 = 100
Step 4: Divide by the sum of weights
Weighted price index = 11800 ÷ 100 = 118
Step 5: Interpret the answer
The overall price index is 118, so the weighted average price level has increased by 18% compared with the base year.

7. Steps for Constructing a Price Index

Base Year

  • Decide which goods and services should be included in the basket.
  • Decide on the base year.
  • Find out the price of each item in the base year.
  • Find out the percentage of income spent on each item.
  • Use spending patterns to determine the weight of each item.
  • Give each item a base year index of 100.

Current Year

  • Find the price of each item in the current year.
  • Calculate the new index for each item.
  • Multiply each new index by its weight.
  • Add the totals.
  • Divide by the sum of the weights.

8. Using a Price Index to Remove the Effects of Inflation

Nominal value: a value measured in current money terms, without adjusting for inflation.
Real value: a value adjusted for inflation.

Data measured in real terms removes the effects of inflation and shows what has happened to the actual quantity or volume of goods and services.

Real value = Nominal value × Base year price index / Current year price index

9. Example: Government Spending in Real Terms

Year Government spending Price index
2017 $500 million 100
2018 $560 million 108
2019 $600 million 112

2018 government spending at 2017 prices

Clicker Example: 2018 Government Spending at 2017 Prices

Government spending in 2018 = $560 million
Base year price index = 100
2018 price index = 108

Step 1: Write the formula
Real value = Nominal value × Base year price index ÷ Current year price index
Step 2: Substitute the numbers
Real government spending = 560 × 100 ÷ 108
Step 3: Calculate
560 × 100 ÷ 108 = 518.52
Step 4: Add units
Real government spending in 2018 = $518.52 million at 2017 prices
Step 5: Interpret
Nominal spending increased by 12%, but real spending increased by only 3.7%.

10. Using Index Numbers to Show Economic Changes

Index numbers can be used to show changes in many economic variables, such as production, exports, consumer spending and real GDP.

Index numbers do not have units. For example, even if the original data is measured in dollars or tonnes, the index number itself has no unit.

11. Example: Real GDP Index

Year Real GDP (£ million) Index of real GDP
2014 1,912,866 97.70
2015 1,957,920 100.00
2016 1,995,478 101.92
2017 2,033,234 103.85
2018 2,061,408 105.29

It is correct to say that real GDP increased by 1.92% between 2015 and 2016 because 2015 is the base year.

It is also correct to say that real GDP increased by 5.29% between 2015 and 2018.

Important:
The change in index points is not always the same as the percentage change.

Calculating percentage change from 2017 to 2018

Percentage change = Change in index / Original index × 100
Percentage change = (105.29 - 103.85) / 103.85 × 100 = 1.39%

Although the index increased by 1.44 points, the percentage increase was 1.39%.

12. Comparing Data at One Point in Time

Index numbers can also be used to compare countries at a single point in time.

  • Country C has labour productivity 13% higher than Country B.
  • Country D has labour productivity 27% higher than Country B.

14. Common Mistakes

Mistake 1:
Thinking that index point change is always the same as percentage change.
Mistake 2:
Forgetting to divide by the sum of weights when calculating a weighted index.
Mistake 3:
Confusing nominal values with real values.
Mistake 4:
Saying nominal spending has increased without checking whether real spending has increased.
