2. What are Macroeconomic Objectives?

Definition:
Macroeconomic objectives are the goals that governments aim to achieve in order to improve economic performance and economic welfare.

Governments try to improve the overall performance of the economy. To do this, they use different macroeconomic policies to achieve a range of economic objectives.

However, governments cannot usually achieve all objectives perfectly at the same time. As a result, policymakers must make choices and trade-offs.

3. Main Macroeconomic Objectives

3.1 Economic Growth

Definition:
Economic growth means an increase in real GDP over time.

Economic growth is important because it may increase incomes, employment and living standards.

Example:
If real GDP rises from $800bn to $840bn, the economy has experienced economic growth.
Economic growth may also create problems such as inflation, environmental damage and current account deficits.

3.2 Price Stability

Definition:
Price stability means keeping inflation low and stable.

Stable prices help consumers and firms plan for the future with greater confidence.

Example:
Central banks often target inflation around 2%.
Very low inflation may support confidence, but deflation can reduce spending and investment.

3.3 Minimising Unemployment

Definition:
Unemployment occurs when people who are willing and able to work cannot find a job.

Low unemployment improves living standards and reduces government welfare spending.

However, extremely low unemployment may increase wage pressure and inflation.

3.4 Stable Balance of Payments on Current Account

Definition:
The current account records trade in goods, trade in services, primary income and secondary income between a country and the rest of the world.

Governments usually want to avoid very large current account deficits.

Example:
Rapid economic growth may increase imports and worsen the current account balance.

4. Other Economic Objectives

4.1 Balancing the Government Budget

Definition:
A balanced budget occurs when government spending equals tax revenue.

Persistent budget deficits may increase national debt.

4.2 Reducing Inequality

Definition:
Inequality refers to an uneven distribution of income and wealth.

Governments may use progressive taxation and welfare spending to reduce inequality.

However, higher taxes may reduce incentives to work and invest.

4.3 Protecting the Environment

Definition:
Environmental protection means reducing the negative impact of economic activity on the natural environment.

Economic growth may increase pollution, resource depletion and carbon emissions.

5. Macroeconomic Policies

5.1 Fiscal Policy

Definition:
Fiscal policy is the use of government spending, taxation and the budget balance to influence the economy.
Government spending ↑ / Tax ↓ → AD ↑ → Real GDP ↑ → Unemployment ↓

Expansionary fiscal policy aims to increase aggregate demand.

Expansionary fiscal policy may increase inflationary pressure and government debt.

5.2 Monetary Policy

Definition:
Monetary policy is the use of interest rates, money supply and credit by the central bank to influence the economy.
Interest rates ↓ → Consumption and Investment ↑ → AD ↑

Lower interest rates encourage borrowing and spending.

Monetary policy may become less effective if confidence is low.

5.3 Supply-side Policies

Definition:
Supply-side policies aim to increase the productive potential of the economy.
Productivity ↑ → LRAS shifts right → Long-run growth ↑

Supply-side policies include education, infrastructure and deregulation.

Supply-side policies may take a long time to have an effect.

6. Conflicts Between Objectives

6.1 Growth vs Inflation

AD ↑ → Real GDP ↑ → Price level ↑

Higher economic growth may create inflationary pressure, especially when the economy is close to full capacity.

6.2 Growth vs Current Account

Economic growth ↑ → Income ↑ → Imports ↑ → Current account deficit ↑

Rising incomes often increase demand for imported goods and services.

6.3 Growth vs Environment

Output ↑ → Pollution ↑ → Environmental damage ↑

Economic growth may increase resource use and environmental problems.

7. Short Run vs Long Run

Short Run

In the short run, demand-side policies may create conflicts between objectives.

Example:
Expansionary fiscal policy may reduce unemployment but increase inflation.

Long Run

In the long run, supply-side improvements may reduce these conflicts.

Example:
Higher productivity may increase growth without causing strong inflationary pressure.

8. It Depends on Conditions

Spare Capacity

If the economy has spare capacity, AD may increase output without much inflation.

Cause of Growth

Growth caused by higher productivity is more sustainable than growth caused purely by rising AD.

Global Economy

Strong global demand may increase exports and improve growth.

Time Period

Objectives may conflict in the short run but become compatible in the long run.

9. Importance Attached to Different Objectives

Governments may prioritise different objectives depending on economic conditions and political beliefs.

During high inflation:
Governments may prioritise price stability and use contractionary policies.
During recession:
Governments may prioritise growth and employment using expansionary policies.
During environmental concerns:
Governments may prioritise sustainability using green taxes and regulation.

Lesson Summary

  • Governments use macroeconomic policies to achieve economic objectives.
  • Main objectives include growth, low inflation, low unemployment and a stable current account.
  • Fiscal, monetary and supply-side policies influence the economy in different ways.
  • Macroeconomic objectives may conflict with each other.
  • Whether objectives conflict often depends on conditions and time period.
  • The importance attached to different objectives affects policy choices.
