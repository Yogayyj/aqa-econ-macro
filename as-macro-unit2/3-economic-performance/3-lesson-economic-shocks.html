Lesson 3: How Demand-side and Supply-side Shocks Affect Economic Activity

This third lesson on economic growth and the cycle explains how internal and external shocks can move an economy into recession, recovery or boom.

1. Economic Shocks

Economic shocks are sudden, unexpected changes that affect aggregate demand or aggregate supply. They are an important reason why economic activity fluctuates around the long-run trend rate of growth.

Demand-side shock: an unexpected change in one or more components of aggregate demand, such as consumption, investment, government spending or exports.
Supply-side shock: an unexpected change in production costs or productive capacity that shifts short-run aggregate supply or long-run aggregate supply.
Where the shock startsDemand-side examplesSupply-side examples
Domestic economyA sudden fall in consumer confidence, producer confidence, credit availability or government spending.Industrial action, domestic energy shortages, or changes in productivity.
Global economyA fall in world growth that reduces export demand.A rise in world oil prices, commodity price shocks, or major technological innovation.

2. Domestic Demand-side Shocks

A stable, growing economy can move into recession if aggregate demand suddenly falls. For example, if households and firms become pessimistic, households may reduce spending on consumer durables and firms may cut investment.

The initial fall in spending causes a downward multiplier effect, so national income falls by more than the original reduction in aggregate demand. Falling income can also create an accelerator effect: firms no longer need to expand capacity, so investment falls further.

Negative domestic demand-side shock

Get it right: the impact of both positive and negative demand-side shocks is amplified by multiplier and accelerator effects.

When confidence returns, aggregate demand starts to rise. The multiplier and accelerator can then help move the economy into recovery and, if demand grows too quickly, into boom.

3. External Demand-side Shocks

Demand-side shocks can also start in the global economy. For many countries, exports are an important component of aggregate demand. If world growth slows, foreign demand for exports may fall, reducing domestic aggregate demand and economic activity.

Negative external demand shock

Lower global growth reduces exports. Since exports are an injection into the circular flow, the fall in exports can create a downward multiplier effect and may cause recession.

Positive external demand shock

Higher global growth increases exports. This raises aggregate demand and can help a recovery or push the economy into a boom.

4. Supply-side Shocks: Oil Prices and SRAS

Supply-side shocks shift aggregate supply. A rise in the world price of oil is a negative supply-side shock for oil-consuming economies because oil is used to produce and transport many goods and services.

A rise in oil prices reduces economic activity in oil-consuming countries

Higher oil prices raise firms' costs, shifting SRAS left. The result is lower real national income and a higher price level. This means a negative supply-side shock can cause both recessionary pressure and inflationary pressure.

Get it right: global events do not affect every country in the same way. Higher oil prices reduce activity in oil-consuming economies but can raise export revenue, aggregate demand and economic activity in oil-producing economies.

5. Case Study: The 1973 Oil Crisis

In October 1973, OPEC restricted oil supply to countries that had supported Israel in the Yom Kippur War. The price of oil rose sharply, creating the first oil shock.

Many countries were already experiencing high inflation, and the increase in oil prices made inflation worse. It also contributed to recession in oil-importing economies. In the UK, the recession lasted from 1973 to 1975, real GDP fell by over 3%, and unemployment continued to rise afterwards.

  1. Explain what is meant by a recession.
  2. Explain why a rise in oil prices can increase both inflation and unemployment.
  3. Explain why unemployment often continues to rise after a recovery begins.

6. Positive Supply-side Shocks and the Kondratieff Wave

A positive supply-side shock can increase economic activity by increasing productivity, reducing costs and shifting aggregate supply to the right. Innovation can also create new products, increasing aggregate demand.

Nikolai Kondratieff argued that major inventions and waves of innovation can create long periods of rising economic activity. Examples include the steam engine, railway systems, information technology and biotechnology. He suggested that these long cycles, known as Kondratieff Waves, may last between 40 and 60 years.

7. Are Fluctuations Only Caused by Shocks?

Shocks are important, but they are not the only cause of fluctuations. Booms can end because real GDP growth is limited by productive capacity. If aggregate demand grows too fast, inflation rises, confidence falls and aggregate demand may decline.

Depressions can also come to an end without a new external shock. Eventually, capital and consumer durables must be replaced, causing investment and consumption to rise. This can trigger multiplier effects and begin recovery.
