Lesson 5: Inflation and Deflation

Distinguish changes in the price level from changes in the inflation rate, then trace domestic, monetary and external causes.

Mind Map Overview

Changes in the price level can originate from aggregate demand, aggregate supply or external forces.

Definitions

  • Inflation: prices rise
  • Disinflation: inflation rate falls
  • Deflation: prices fall

Demand-pull

  • AD outgrows capacity
  • Positive output gap
  • Excess money growth can raise AD

Cost-push

  • Input and wage costs rise
  • SRAS shifts left
  • Output falls as price level rises

External channels

  • World commodity prices
  • Exchange-rate pass-through
  • Deflationary policy reduces AD

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish inflation, deflation and disinflation precisely.
  • Analyse demand-pull and cost-push inflation using AD/AS.
  • Explain how money-supply growth, commodity prices and exchange rates affect inflation.
  • Distinguish a deflationary policy from an outcome of deflation.

1. Three essential definitions

ConceptMeaningExample
InflationA sustained rise in the general price level.Inflation falls from 6% to 3%: prices are still rising.
DisinflationA fall in the positive rate of inflation.The price index rises more slowly than before.
DeflationA sustained fall in the general price level; inflation is negative.An index falls from 110 to 108.
Get it right: falling inflation does not mean falling prices. Only deflation means the general price level is falling.

2. Demand-pull inflation

Demand-pull inflation occurs when aggregate demand grows faster than productive capacity, especially near full employment. Higher consumption, investment, government spending or net exports shifts AD right, increasing real output and the price level in the short run.

AD rises→positive output gap / scarce resources→wages and prices rise

Money supply: excessive monetary growth can lower borrowing costs and increase liquidity, consumption and investment. If spending rises faster than potential output, AD rises and causes inflation. The size and timing depend on money demand, confidence, credit creation and spare capacity.

3. Cost-push inflation

Cost-push inflation occurs when production costs rise, shifting SRAS left. The price level rises while real output falls, creating a difficult policy trade-off.

Wages

Wage growth above productivity growth raises unit labour costs.

Commodities

Higher world oil, food or metals prices increase input and transport costs.

Taxes and supply

Indirect taxes, disrupted supply chains or poor harvests can raise firms' costs.

Evaluation: the inflation effect depends on the commodity's importance, firms' ability to absorb margins, the exchange rate, and whether expectations cause second-round wage and price increases.

4. Exchange-rate transmission

Depreciation

Imports cost more in domestic currency, producing imported cost-push inflation. Exports become more price competitive, so net exports and AD may rise, adding demand-pull pressure.

Appreciation

Cheaper imports reduce input and consumer prices, while weaker net exports can reduce AD. Inflationary pressure is likely to fall.

Pass-through may be incomplete or delayed because firms hedge currencies, use inventories or change profit margins.

5. Deflation and deflationary policy

Deflationary policy means fiscal or monetary policy designed to reduce aggregate demand and inflationary pressure. It does not necessarily cause deflation: inflation might simply fall from 7% to 2%.

Deflation caused by weak demand can postpone consumption, raise the real value of debt and reduce firms' revenue, creating a damaging cycle of lower spending and unemployment. Deflation caused by productivity growth may be less harmful because output and real incomes can rise while prices fall.

Exam practice: Use AD/AS to compare the effect of a 25% rise in world oil prices with excessive growth of the domestic money supply. Which is harder for policymakers to address, and why?

Check your understanding

  1. If inflation falls from 5% to 2%, is this deflation? Explain.
  2. Give two causes of demand-pull inflation and two causes of cost-push inflation.
  3. Explain two channels through which depreciation may increase inflation.
  4. Why might higher commodity prices have different effects across countries?
  5. Why need a deflationary policy not produce deflation?
