Lesson 1: Economic Growth and the Economic Cycle

Understand the difference between short-run and long-run economic growth, the demand-side and supply-side causes of growth, and how economists illustrate growth using production possibility diagrams.

Learning Objectives

  • Explain the difference between short-run and long-run economic growth.
  • Calculate the annual percentage change in real GDP.
  • Use production possibility diagrams to distinguish actual growth from growth in productive capacity.
  • Analyse demand-side determinants of short-run growth and supply-side determinants of the long-run trend rate.
  • Explain how export-led growth can affect employment, aggregate demand and productive capacity.

1. Short-run and Long-run Economic Growth

Short-run growth

Short-run economic growth is the percentage change in the total output of an economy from one period to the next, usually measured over one year. It is also called the actual rate of economic growth.

Growth rate = ((real GDP in year 2 − real GDP in year 1) ÷ real GDP in year 1) × 100

Example: if real GDP rises from $500bn to $525bn, growth is 5%.

Long-run growth

Long-run economic growth is the average annual percentage change in an economy's productive capacity over several years. It is also called the underlying or trend rate of growth.

Because productive capacity is difficult to measure directly, economists often estimate trend growth by averaging short-run growth over long periods, such as 10, 20 or 50 years.

Key distinction: short-run growth is about a rise in actual national output; long-run growth is about a rise in the economy's potential output or productive capacity.

2. Illustrating Growth with Production Possibility Diagrams

A production possibility boundary (PPB) shows the combinations of goods and services an economy can produce when resources are fully and efficiently employed with current technology.

Short-run growth

The economy moves from inside the PPB to a point closer to it. Output rises because spare capacity is used more fully.

Long-run growth

The PPB shifts right because productive capacity increases through more resources, higher productivity or better technology.

Both types together

Supply-side improvements shift the PPB outwards, while rising aggregate demand moves the economy to a higher actual output.

Get it right

When a PPB is used for the whole macroeconomy, label the axes with economy-wide outputs such as goods and services, consumer and capital goods, or public and private sector goods.

3. Demand-side Determinants of Short-run Growth

In the short run, economic growth depends mainly on the growth of aggregate demand. If aggregate demand rises, firms increase output and employ more factors of production, causing real GDP to rise.

ComponentHow it can increase short-run growth
ConsumptionHigher consumer confidence or income can increase spending, encouraging firms to produce more.
InvestmentHigher producer confidence can increase investment, which raises aggregate demand immediately and may also create capacity.
Government spendingFiscal expansion can boost demand, especially during recession or when private-sector demand is weak.
ExportsExport sales are an injection into the circular flow and can create multiplier effects on output and employment.

Short-run growth is limited by productive capacity. Once an economy approaches normal capacity, extra demand is more likely to raise the price level than real output.

Negative short-run economic growth: a fall in real GDP, usually because aggregate demand falls and firms reduce output to avoid unsold stocks.

4. Supply-side Determinants of Long-run Growth

The long-run trend rate of growth is mainly determined by improvements in the supply-side performance of the economy.

Private-sector sources

  • Investment in new capital equipment.
  • Research and development leading to technological change.
  • Training that improves labour productivity.
  • Innovation that creates new and better products.

Government sources

  • Infrastructure spending that reduces journey times and improves efficiency.
  • Education and training that increase skills and adaptability.
  • Funding research and development.
  • Supply-side policies that improve incentives, competition and productivity.

Aggregate demand can still influence long-run growth. If demand rises steadily, firms may earn higher profits, invest in capacity and spend more on training and research. If demand is too weak, investment may fall; if demand is too high, inflation and balance of payments problems can damage investment.

5. Export-led Growth: China Case Study

Export-led growth occurs when a significant part of the increase in a country's real GDP results from selling goods and services abroad.

Exports supported the development of several economies after the Second World War, including China, Germany, Ireland, Japan, Mexico, Singapore and South Korea. China's export growth accelerated after joining the World Trade Organisation in 2001. Rapid export growth increased aggregate demand, created jobs and encouraged both private and government investment.

Case study questions

  1. Explain why rising exports can increase employment and short-run economic growth.
  2. Explain why higher exports may also increase productive capacity.
  3. Explain how a change in net exports affects aggregate demand.

Progress Questions
