Lesson 6: The Balance of Payments on Current Account

Understand why economies trade, classify current-account flows and analyse the forces behind deficits and surpluses.

Learning objectives

  • Explain why international trade matters for consumers, firms and macroeconomic performance.
  • Recognise the balance of payments as a record of transactions with the rest of the world.
  • Classify current-account transactions into four components.
  • Calculate and interpret a current-account deficit or surplus.
  • Analyse productivity, inflation, exchange-rate and activity effects.

1. Why international trade matters

Specialisation

Countries can specialise according to comparative advantage, raising total output and consumption possibilities.

Competition and scale

Larger markets can create economies of scale, innovation and competitive pressure.

Choice and inputs

Imports provide consumers with variety and firms with raw materials, capital goods and technology.

Trade also creates adjustment costs: import competition may displace workers, exposure to global shocks may rise and gains may be unevenly distributed.

2. The balance of payments

Balance of payments: a record of a country's financial transactions with the rest of the world over a period of time.

The current account is only one part of this record. The capital and financial accounts and balancing item are studied later in Unit 4; do not describe the current account as the whole balance of payments.

Current-account componentCredits (+)Debits (−)
Trade in goodsExports of physical productsImports of physical products
Trade in servicesServices sold abroad, such as tourism, finance or educationServices purchased from abroad
Primary incomeIncome from overseas employment and investmentsIncome paid to overseas workers and investors
Secondary incomeCurrent transfers received without a direct returnAid, remittances or other current transfers paid abroad

Current-account balance = goods + services + primary income + secondary income

3. Deficit and surplus

Current-account deficit

Total current-account debits exceed credits. It is not automatically harmful: imports of capital goods may support future capacity. Persistent deficits may, however, reflect weak competitiveness or reliance on external finance.

Current-account surplus

Total credits exceed debits. This may reflect competitiveness and saving, but can also accompany weak domestic demand and may contribute to international imbalances.

Exam technique: do not call every trade deficit a current-account deficit. Income and transfer balances may offset the trade balance.

4. What changes the balance?

FactorLikely transmissionWhy the outcome is uncertain
ProductivityLower unit costs and improved non-price competitiveness can raise exports and reduce import penetration.Benefits may feed into wages rather than prices; quality and reliability also matter.
Domestic inflationInflation above trading partners makes exports less competitive and imports relatively attractive.Exchange-rate movements or differentiated products may offset the effect.
DepreciationExports become cheaper abroad and imports dearer domestically, tending to improve net trade.Depends on demand elasticities, contracts, imported inputs and time lags; the value may initially worsen.
Domestic activityHigher domestic income increases demand for imports, tending to weaken the current account.The import propensity determines the size.
Activity abroadHigher foreign incomes increase export demand, tending to strengthen the current account.Depends on export markets and competitiveness.

5. A complete analysis chain

productivity rises→unit costs fall / quality rises→exports rise and import demand falls→current account improves

Always distinguish a change in an export's price from a change in export revenue. After depreciation, export revenue only rises if the quantity response is strong enough. Responses are often small in the short run and larger once buyers can switch suppliers.

Calculation: goods −£120bn, services +£75bn, primary income +£8bn and secondary income −£17bn. Calculate the current-account balance, identify deficit/surplus, and explain one factor that could improve it.

Check your understanding

  2. Why is the current account not the same as the balance of payments?
  3. Explain how faster domestic growth may worsen the current account.
  4. Why might depreciation fail to improve the balance immediately?
  5. Evaluate whether a current-account deficit is always a problem.
