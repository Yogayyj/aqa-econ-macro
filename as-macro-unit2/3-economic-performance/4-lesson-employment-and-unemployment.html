1. Measuring unemployment

ILO unemployment: people aged 16 or over who are without a job, are available to start work and have actively sought work in the recent past (or are waiting to start an agreed job). The measure comes from a household labour-force survey, so claiming benefits is not required.

Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ economically active × 100
Economically active = employed + unemployed

ILO measure

Internationally comparable and captures jobseekers who do not claim benefits. It is survey-based, so it is subject to sampling and response error.

Claimant count

Counts people receiving specified unemployment-related benefits. It is timely administrative data, but eligibility and benefit rules can change and not every unemployed person claims.

Exam trap: economically inactive people—such as students or retirees who are not seeking work—are neither employed nor unemployed. A falling unemployment rate can therefore conceal rising inactivity.

2. Four types of unemployment

TypeCause and exampleMost suitable response
Cyclical (demand-deficient)Weak aggregate demand in a downturn reduces derived demand for labour. It is associated with a negative output gap.Expansionary fiscal or monetary policy, if spare capacity and inflation conditions allow.
StructuralA mismatch between workers' skills/location and available vacancies, perhaps after deindustrialisation or technological change.Training, education, mobility support, infrastructure and improved labour-market information.
FrictionalShort-term unemployment while people enter the workforce or search between jobs.Better job matching and information; some frictional unemployment can improve matches and productivity.
SeasonalPredictable changes in labour demand across the year, for example tourism or agriculture.Diversification, retraining and flexible work; use seasonally adjusted data when comparing months.

3. Unemployment versus underemployment

Unemployment: a person has no job but is available for and actively seeking work.
Underemployment: a person is employed but wants more hours, or works below their skills/qualifications.

Headline unemployment can fall while labour resources remain underused. Involuntary part-time work, insecure hours and graduate skill mismatch may mean actual output remains below potential output.

4. Demand-side, supply-side and global causes

AD falls→firms' sales fall→derived demand for labour falls→cyclical unemployment rises

A negative output gap means actual output is below productive capacity, so cyclical unemployment is likely. By contrast, supply-side barriers—skills mismatch, immobility, weak incentives or labour-market discrimination—raise the economy's normal level of unemployment and reduce effective capacity, placing LRAS further left than it otherwise would be.

Trade and offshoring

Export growth creates jobs, but import competition and relocation can cause structural unemployment in exposed regions.

Migration

Can fill shortages, increase productive capacity and create demand; effects vary by skills, location and time period.

Global shocks

Foreign recessions reduce exports, while commodity shocks, automation and global supply-chain changes alter sectoral labour demand.

5. Policy depends on the diagnosis

Demand stimulus can reduce cyclical unemployment, but may cause inflation if the output gap is small. Supply-side policy is slower but better targeted at structural causes. Cutting benefits may strengthen job-search incentives, yet can increase poverty and will not create vacancies during a recession.

Exam practice: A country has a negative output gap, high long-term unemployment in former manufacturing regions and many unfilled digital vacancies. Explain why one policy alone is unlikely to solve its unemployment problem. Include an AD/AS chain and one evaluation point.
