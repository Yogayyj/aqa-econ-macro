Lesson 2: The Economic Cycle and Output Gaps

This second lesson on economic growth and the cycle explains booms, recessions, depressions and recoveries, then shows how real GDP, unemployment, inflation and investment help identify each phase.

Learning Objectives

  • Define the economic cycle and its four main phases.
  • Explain the characteristics of a boom, recession, depression and recovery.
  • Use real GDP, unemployment, inflation and investment as indicators of the economic cycle.
  • Distinguish between positive and negative output gaps.
  • Explain the likely consequences of persistent output gaps.

1. What is the Economic Cycle?

Economies do not grow at the same rate every year. Most economies experience fluctuations in economic activity around their long-run trend rate of economic growth. These fluctuations are known as the economic cycle.

Expansion: a period when aggregate demand, real output and employment are increasing.
Contraction: a period when aggregate demand, real output and employment are falling.

Economic cycle: actual GDP around trend GDP

The trend GDP line slopes upward because the productive capacity of the economy normally increases over time.

2. The Four Main Phases

Boom

Output is above normal capacity. Aggregate demand is very high, unemployment is low, inflation is high and rising, wages rise quickly and firms may find it difficult to recruit workers.

Recession

Aggregate demand and economic activity are falling. Output falls, unemployment rises, wage growth slows and firms make lower profits. A technical recession means two consecutive quarters of negative real GDP growth.

Depression

A severe or prolonged slump. Households and firms are pessimistic, aggregate demand is low, spare capacity is high, unemployment is high, wages may be cut and deflation may occur.

Recovery

Real GDP begins to rise after recession or depression. Firms increase output, unemployment starts to fall, and confidence, wages, inflation and investment may gradually rise.

Exam tip: an economy does not always pass through every phase. For example, it may recover from recession without entering a depression.

3. Indicators Used to Identify the Cycle

IndicatorWhat it showsTypical pattern over the cycle
Real GDPThe main measure of national output and economic activity.Falls in recession, may remain far below capacity in depression, rises in recovery and is constrained by trend growth in a boom.
UnemploymentShows the unused labour resources in the economy.Usually rises after output starts falling and falls after recovery begins, so it is a lagging indicator.
InflationShows pressure on the price level.Usually rises in a boom, falls during recession, and may become deflation in a depression.
InvestmentThe most volatile component of aggregate demand.Falls sharply when demand and profits fall; rises in a boom when firms need more capacity.
Lagging indicator: a statistic that changes after an event has taken place. Unemployment is often lagging because firms usually wait before hiring or dismissing workers.
Time lag: the period between one event and another, such as the delay between falling output and rising unemployment.

4. Positive and Negative Output Gaps

An output gap exists when the economy's equilibrium level of national income is not equal to its normal capacity level of national income, also called its productive potential.

Output gap = equilibrium national income − normal capacity national income = Ye − YN

Positive output gap

Equilibrium national income is greater than normal capacity. This is associated with a boom and can cause accelerating inflation and a worsening current account if it persists.

Negative output gap

Equilibrium national income is less than normal capacity. This is associated with recession or depression and can cause disinflation, deflation and high unemployment if it persists.

Positive output gap: Ye > YN

Negative output gap: Ye < YN

5. Output Gaps on the Economic Cycle Diagram

The vertical distance between actual GDP and trend GDP shows the size of the output gap. If actual GDP is above trend GDP, the economy has a positive output gap. If actual GDP is below trend GDP, the economy has a negative output gap.
