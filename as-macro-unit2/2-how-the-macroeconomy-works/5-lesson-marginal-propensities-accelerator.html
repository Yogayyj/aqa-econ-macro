Lesson 5: Marginal Propensities, Saving, Investment and the Accelerator

This lesson extends aggregate demand by showing how changes in income affect saving, taxation and imports, before explaining the difference between saving and investment and the basic accelerator process.

← Back to Module 2 lessons

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

  • define the marginal propensities to consume, save, tax and import;
  • calculate changes in saving, tax revenue and imports when national income changes;
  • explain why saving and investment are different in macroeconomics;
  • use the marginal capital-output ratio to explain the accelerator process; and
  • evaluate the limits of the accelerator theory.

The Marginal Propensities to Consume, Save, Tax and Import

As explained earlier, the marginal propensity to consume (MPC) measures how a change in income affects consumption. The other marginal propensities show how a change in income affects saving, taxation and imports.

Marginal Propensity to Save (MPS)

The marginal propensity to save measures the proportion of a change in income that is saved.

MPS = ∆S / ∆Y

If the MPS is 0.15 and national income rises by $200 million, saving will increase by $30 million.

∆S = ∆Y × MPS

MPS is not the same as the savings ratio. MPS measures the effect of a change in income, while the savings ratio measures the proportion of total disposable income saved.

Marginal Propensity to Tax (MPT)

The marginal propensity to tax, also known as the marginal rate of taxation, measures how a change in income affects tax revenue.

MPT = ∆T / ∆Y

If national income falls by $500 million and the MPT is 0.3, tax revenue will fall by $150 million.

∆T = ∆Y × MPT

Marginal Propensity to Import (MPM)

The marginal propensity to import measures how a change in income affects spending on imports.

MPM = ∆M / ∆Y

If national income increases by $400 million and the MPM is 0.25, spending on imports will increase by $100 million.

∆M = ∆Y × MPM

How the Marginal Propensities Add Up

In a closed economy without a government sector, MPC + MPS must equal 1. Any increase in income that is not consumed must be saved.

In an open economy with a government sector, MPC + MPS + MPT + MPM must equal 1. Any change in income must be spent on home-produced goods, saved, taxed or spent on imports.

Key terms: MPS is the proportion of a change in income that is saved; MPT is the proportion of a change in income that is taxed; MPM is the proportion of a change in income that is spent on imports.

Get it right

Marginal propensities measure how a change in income affects consumption, saving, taxation or imports. Do not confuse them with average ratios calculated from total income.

The Difference Between Saving and Investment

Saving is a withdrawal from the circular flow of income. It is the part of households' disposable income that is not spent. People may save by putting money in a bank account, buying shares or contributing to a pension fund.

Financial institutions that receive savings often lend those funds to firms that want to invest in new capital, such as offices, machinery or computers. Investment is an injection into the circular flow of income and increases aggregate demand.

Get it right

In economics, buying shares is a form of saving for households. When a company issues new shares, it may receive funds that can then be used for investment in capital equipment.

The Basic Accelerator Process

The accelerator process is a theory of investment. It is not a complete explanation of what determines investment, but it explains one important influence on investment: changes in national income.

If national income rises, aggregate demand also rises. If firms are close to full capacity, they may need to invest in extra productive capacity to meet the higher demand.

Marginal Capital-Output Ratio (MCOR)

The marginal capital-output ratio is the amount that has to be spent on capital to produce a given increase in annual output.

For example, if an extra $30,000 of machinery raises annual output by $10,000, the MCOR is 3:1.

The MCOR determines the accelerator coefficient. If the MCOR is 4:1, the accelerator coefficient is 4. Because the MCOR is usually greater than 1, an increase in national income often requires a larger increase in investment to allow firms to meet the extra demand.

Investment = α × change in national income, or I = α × ∆Y

The symbol α represents the accelerator coefficient. For example, if the MCOR is 2.5:1 and national income rises by $500 million, the accelerator theory predicts that $1,250 million of extra investment will be required.

Limitations of the Accelerator Theory

  • The theory is most useful when the economy is approaching full capacity. If firms have spare capacity, they may meet extra demand without investing in new capital.
  • Firms will only invest if they expect higher demand to continue. Expectations and business confidence are therefore important.
  • Some investment replaces worn-out or obsolete capital. The accelerator theory does not focus on replacement investment.
Replacement investment: investment used to replace capital that has worn out or become obsolete.

Aggregate Demand and the Level of Economic Activity

Economic activity involves the production, consumption, exchange and distribution of goods and services. Real GDP is the main indicator of economic activity. Employment and unemployment are also useful indicators.

In the short run, aggregate demand is a key determinant of economic activity. When AD rises, firms may increase output and employ more workers. When AD falls, output and employment are likely to fall.

If there is spare capacity, higher AD is likely to raise real output and employment. As the economy approaches full capacity, further increases in AD are more likely to raise the price level instead, creating inflationary pressure.

Progress Questions

  1. What is the difference between saving and investment?
  2. Why is the accelerator coefficient likely to be greater than 1?
  3. In an economy, national income increased by $300 million and the marginal capital-output ratio is 2.5. What amount of investment would be predicted by the accelerator theory?
  4. Why is the accelerator theory unlikely to provide a satisfactory explanation of investment when the economy has plenty of spare capacity?

Lesson Summary

Marginal propensities explain how a change in income is divided between consumption, saving, taxation and imports. Saving is a withdrawal from the circular flow, while investment is an injection. The accelerator theory explains why rising income can lead to a more than proportional increase in investment, especially when firms are near full capacity.
