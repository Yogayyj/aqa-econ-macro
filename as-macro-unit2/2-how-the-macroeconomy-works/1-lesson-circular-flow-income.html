2. What Does National Income Measure?

Definition:
National income measures the total income earned by factors of production in an economy over a period of time.

National income is closely linked to the total value of output produced in the economy. When firms produce goods and services, they pay incomes to households in the form of wages, rent, interest and profit.

National income = Total income earned from producing goods and services
Example:
If firms produce more output, they usually need more labour and other resources. This increases the income paid to households.

2.1 Measuring National Income: Output Method

Output method:
Statisticians estimate output across industries (for example retail, hairdressing, insurance and manufacturing), then add each industry's value added.
Value added = Value of output − Value of intermediate inputs

Value added is used to avoid double counting. If we simply add total sales from every firm, the same input can be counted more than once when it is sold between firms.

Value added in the circular flow: Firms receive spending, transform inputs into final output, and create value added. This value added is distributed as factor incomes (wages, rent, interest and profit), linking output to income.
Quick example:
A bakery sells output worth $500 and buys intermediate inputs worth $220.
Value added = $500 − $220 = $280.
Example:
Sugar is sold to a biscuit firm. If we add both firms' total sales, sugar is counted twice. Using value added at each stage removes this problem.

Output is measured in money terms because money provides a common unit. Different products (such as laptops and tea) cannot be added meaningfully unless converted into monetary value.

2.2 Income Method

Output creates incomes for factors of production. National income can also be calculated by adding up all the income earned by the factors of production.

National income (income method) = wages + rent + interest + profit

The monetary value of output should create an equal amount of income.

2.3 Expenditure Method

Output also equals expenditure because produced output is purchased by households, firms, government or foreign buyers. Stock (inventory) changes are included in investment, so unsold output is treated as firms buying their own output.

Expenditure method = C + I + G + X − M
  • C: household consumption expenditure.
  • I: firms' investment spending, including stock changes.
  • G: government spending on goods and services (excluding transfer payments).
  • X: exports.
  • M: imports (subtracted because they are not domestic output).
In theory all three methods should give the same figure: output = income = expenditure. In practice, statisticians may observe small differences because of data and measurement errors.

2.3.1 Common Question: Why is export positive and import negative?

In the expenditure approach, we count spending on domestic output. Exports (X) are spending on domestically produced goods and services by foreigners, so they are added. Imports (M) are spending on foreign-produced goods and services, so they are subtracted.

Expenditure on domestic output = C + I + G + X − M
Quick intuition:
If households buy imported phones, that spending appears in consumption (C), but it is not domestic output. Subtracting M removes this foreign-output part and keeps the total focused on domestic production.

2.4 The Difference Between GDP, GNP and NNP

Gross domestic product (GDP) measures the value of output produced within a country. Gross national product (GNP) is GDP plus net income from abroad.

GNP = GDP + net income from abroad

Net income from abroad is income earned by domestic residents from abroad minus income earned by foreign residents from the domestic economy. One example is interest earned on money deposited in a foreign bank.

Net national product (NNP) adjusts GNP by subtracting depreciation (capital consumption). Over time, part of output is needed to replace worn-out or obsolete capital.

NNP = GNP − depreciation

National income can also be measured at market prices or factor cost. Indirect taxes raise market prices, while subsidies lower market prices relative to factor cost.

Difference between market price and factor cost:
Market price is what buyers pay in the market (including indirect taxes and the effect of subsidies). Factor cost is what producers receive for supplying factors of production, excluding those tax/subsidy distortions.
National income at factor cost = national income at market prices − indirect taxes + subsidies
Common question: why is tax negative and subsidy positive in this formula?
Tax is negative because indirect taxes are included in market prices but are not income earned by factors of production, so we subtract them.
Subsidy is positive because subsidies reduce market prices below factor earnings, so we add them back to recover factor-cost income.

For example: NNP at factor cost = NNP at market prices − indirect taxes + subsidies. NNP at factor cost is also called net national income (NNI). NNI per capita is often used as an indicator of living standards.

3. Nominal Income and Real Income

3.1 Nominal Income

Definition:
Nominal income is income measured in current money terms, without adjusting for inflation.

Nominal income may rise simply because prices have increased.

3.2 Real Income

Definition:
Real income is income adjusted for inflation.

Real income is more useful for measuring economic performance because it shows whether people can actually buy more goods and services.

Real income = Nominal income / Price index × 100
Example:
If nominal national income rises by 8% but prices rise by 5%, real national income rises by approximately 3%.

4. Real National Income as an Indicator of Economic Performance

Real national income is an important indicator because it measures the value of output after removing the effects of inflation.

If real national income increases, it suggests that the economy is producing more goods and services.

However, real national income does not show income distribution, environmental costs, unpaid work or changes in quality of life.

5. The Circular Flow of Income Model

Households provide factors of production to firms. Firms produce goods and services and pay income to households.

In the simplest circular flow model, there are two main groups:

  • Households own factors of production and receive income.
  • Firms use factors of production to produce goods and services.

Households spend their income on goods and services produced by firms. Firms then use this revenue to pay households for the factors of production.

6. Income = Output = Expenditure

Key idea:
The value of national income, national output and national expenditure should be equal.
Income = Output = Expenditure

This is because one person’s spending becomes another person’s income. When firms sell output, they receive expenditure from households. This revenue is then paid out as income to workers and owners of factors of production.

Example:
A student spends $10 on lunch. This $10 is expenditure for the student, revenue for the firm and income for workers or business owners.

7. Injections and Withdrawals

7.1 Injections

Definition:
Injections are additions of spending into the circular flow of income.
  • Investment: spending by firms on capital goods
  • Government spending: spending by the government on goods and services
  • Exports: spending by foreign consumers and firms on domestic goods and services
Injections = I + G + X

7.2 Withdrawals

Definition:
Withdrawals are leakages of income from the circular flow.
  • Saving: income not spent by households
  • Taxation: income taken by the government
  • Imports: spending on foreign goods and services
Withdrawals = S + T + M

8. Equilibrium Income and Full Employment Income

8.1 Equilibrium Income

Definition:
Equilibrium income is the level of national income where planned injections equal planned withdrawals.
Injections = Withdrawals

At equilibrium, there is no tendency for national income to change.

8.2 Full Employment Income

Definition:
Full employment income is the level of national income where the economy is using its resources fully.

Equilibrium income does not always equal full employment income. An economy can be in equilibrium while still having unemployment.

If equilibrium income is below full employment income, there is likely to be a negative output gap and cyclical unemployment.

8.3 Equilibrium Income in a Two-Sector Economy (Households + Firms)

Key terms:
Withdrawal (leakage): income not spent on domestic goods and services.
Injection: spending added to the circular flow.
Closed economy: no international trade (no exports or imports).

In a two-sector model, aggregate demand is:

AD = C + I

If households save part of their income, consumption alone may be too small to buy all output. Investment spending by firms is an injection that adds demand. National income is in equilibrium when output sold equals output produced, so income is stable from one period to the next.

C + I = Y   and   S = I

8.4 Two Disequilibrium Cases in a Two-Sector Economy

If planned spending is not equal to output, firms will change production and income will adjust.

Case 1 (contraction): when withdrawals are greater than injections (S > I), aggregate demand is below current output, firms accumulate unsold stocks and reduce production.

Case 2 (expansion): when injections are greater than withdrawals (I > S), aggregate demand is above current output and firms increase production.

8.5 Equilibrium Income with Government and Foreign Trade (Open Economy)

Key term: Open economy = an economy that trades with other economies (exports and imports).

With government and foreign trade included, withdrawals become saving, taxation and imports, while injections become investment, government spending and exports.

Withdrawals: S + T + M   |   Injections: I + G + X

Aggregate demand can be written as:

AD = C + I + G + X - M

(or equivalently AD = C + I + G + (X - M)).

Equilibrium conditions: AD = Y   and   I + G + X = S + T + M

9. Effects of Changes in Injections and Withdrawals

When injections increase

I / G / X ↑ → AD ↑ → National income ↑ → Employment ↑

An increase in injections adds extra spending to the circular flow. This increases aggregate demand and can increase national income.

When withdrawals increase

S / T / M ↑ → Spending in circular flow ↓ → National income ↓

An increase in withdrawals removes spending from the circular flow. This can reduce aggregate demand and lower national income.

The final effect depends on the size of the change, the multiplier, spare capacity and business confidence.

10. Clicker Example: Equilibrium Income

Example

In an economy:
Investment = $40bn
Government spending = $60bn
Exports = $30bn
Saving = $50bn
Taxation = $55bn
Imports = $25bn

Step 1: Calculate injections
I + G + X = 40 + 60 + 30 = $130bn
Step 2: Calculate withdrawals
S + T + M = 50 + 55 + 25 = $130bn
Step 3: Compare injections and withdrawals
Injections = Withdrawals
Step 4: Interpret
The economy is in equilibrium because planned injections equal planned withdrawals.
