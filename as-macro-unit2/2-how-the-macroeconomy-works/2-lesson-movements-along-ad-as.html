Lesson 2: Movements Along the AD and AS Curves

Spec focus (3.2.2.2): why changes in the price level are represented by movements along AD and AS curves.

Before the Curves: What AD and AS Mean

Aggregate demand (AD) is the world’s total spending on this country’s final output at different price levels.

Link to the previous lesson (flow of income): AD is the demand-side mirror of income flows in the circular flow — money spent on final output becomes income for domestic producers/factor owners.

AD identity: C + I + G + (X − M)
Spending SourceWhy included in AD?
Consumption (C)Households demand goods and services produced in the domestic economy.
Investment (I)Firms demand capital goods (e.g. machinery, buildings, software, inventories).
Government spending (G)Government buys goods and services, adding to demand for domestic output.
Exports (X)Foreign buyers demand domestically produced output.
Imports (M)Subtracted because this spending is on foreign-produced goods, not domestic output.

Why these components? Together, they capture all major spending injections into demand for domestically produced final goods and services.

Aggregate supply (AS) is total output that firms are willing and able to produce at different price levels.

  • In the short run, firms respond to profitability and production costs.
  • In the long run, supply depends on productive capacity: labour, capital, resources, and productivity.
Exam tip: AD is about planned spending; AS is about planned output.

Movements Along the AD Curve

The aggregate demand (AD) curve shows the inverse relationship between the price level and real national income demanded.

Key idea: A fall in the price level causes a movement along AD, increasing aggregate demand (ceteris paribus).
逻辑链对比（Micro vs Macro）
  • 微观 Demand：一个商品价格变化 → quantity demanded 变化
  • 宏观 AD：整个 economy price level 变化 → total spending 变化 → national output/income 变化

This is why the AD curve links price level to real national income demanded, rather than to the quantity of a single product.

  • Competitiveness effect: lower domestic prices can increase exports and reduce imports.
  • Real wealth effect: lower prices raise the real value of money balances and fixed-value assets, supporting spending.

So, movement from a higher price level (PL1) to a lower one (PL2) means output demanded rises from Y1 to Y2.

AD curve (movement along)

AS Curve Shape: Horizontal, Upward Sloping, and Vertical

In many textbook diagrams, AS can be shown with three zones to explain spare capacity and full capacity.

AS shape across different capacity conditions
  • Horizontal section (low output): when there is a lot of spare capacity, firms can raise output without higher prices.
  • Upward-sloping section: as capacity is used up, higher prices/profit incentives are needed for additional output.
  • Vertical section (full capacity, YF): output cannot rise further in the short run, so extra demand mainly raises the price level.
Key interpretation: At full capacity, short-run output is capped. In the long run, only growth in productive capacity can shift this limit rightward.

Movements Along the AS Curve

The aggregate supply (AS) curve shows the direct relationship between the price level and real output supplied.

Key idea: A change in the price level causes a movement along AS, not a shift.

If price rises, production tends to be more profitable, so firms increase output. If price falls, firms reduce output.

Exam tip: “Movement along” means only the price level changed. All other determinants are held constant.
AS curve (movement along)

Factors that Shift the AD Curve and the Short-Run Aggregate Supply (SRAS) Curve

Shifts happen when non-price-level factors change (so this is different from a movement along a curve).

AD Curve Shifts

An AD shift happens when non-price determinants of spending change.

  • Consumer confidence
  • Business confidence and investment
  • Taxes on income
  • Government spending
  • World growth and exports

Right shift = more demand at every price level. Left shift = less demand at every price level.

Rightward shift of AD

SRAS Curve Shifts

SRAS shifts mainly because of changes in costs of production.

  • Raw material and energy prices
  • Wages
  • Indirect taxes and subsidies
  • Productivity
Rule: Rising costs shift SRAS left (less output at any given price level). Falling costs shift SRAS right.
Leftward shift of SRAS (cost rise)
