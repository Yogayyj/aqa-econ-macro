Lesson 7.3: Determinants of Aggregate Demand

Class Main Points for A-Level Economics

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, students should be able to explain:

  • the meaning of aggregate demand
  • the components of aggregate demand
  • the determinants of consumption, investment, government spending, exports and imports
  • the determinants of savings
  • how changes in these factors shift aggregate demand

Mind Map Overview

Aggregate demand changes when any component of AD changes: consumption, investment, government spending, exports or imports.
Consumption and Savings

Disposable income

Higher post-tax income usually raises consumption and can shift AD right.

Confidence and wealth

Optimism or rising asset prices encourage households to spend more.

Savings

Higher saving reduces current consumption, lowering AD in the short run.

Investment

Interest rates

Lower borrowing costs make more investment projects profitable.

Business confidence

Expected future demand influences firms' willingness to invest.

Technology

Innovation can create profitable opportunities for capital spending.

Government Spending

Fiscal policy

More public spending directly increases G and shifts AD right.

Taxation

Lower income tax can raise disposable income and consumption.

Budget priorities

Spending decisions may target infrastructure, welfare, health or education.

Exports and Imports

Exchange rate

Depreciation can make exports cheaper and imports dearer, raising net exports.

World income

Stronger overseas growth increases demand for exports.

Import spending

Higher imports are a withdrawal from domestic AD, reducing net exports.

Class Main Points

1. Aggregate Demand

Aggregate demand is the total spending on goods and services in an economy at a given price level over a period of time.

AD = C + I + G + (X − M)

  • C = Consumption
  • I = Investment
  • G = Government spending
  • X = Exports
  • M = Imports

If consumption, investment, government spending or exports increase, aggregate demand is likely to increase. If imports increase, aggregate demand may fall because spending leaves the domestic economy.

2. Determinants of Consumption

Consumption is household spending on goods and services. It is often the largest component of aggregate demand in many economies.

Income

Income is one of the most important determinants of consumption. Other things being equal, when national income increases, household consumption is likely to increase.

The marginal propensity to consume, or MPC, measures the proportion of a change in income that is spent on consumption.

MPC = Change in Consumption ÷ Change in Income

Example: If national income increases by $100 million and MPC is 0.6, consumption will increase by $60 million.

Change in Consumption = MPC × Change in Income

Wealth

Wealth refers to the value of assets owned by households, such as houses, savings and shares. If households become wealthier, they may feel more financially secure and increase consumption.

For example, rising house prices may make households feel richer, so they may spend more on consumer durables such as cars, furniture and washing machines.

Taxation

Taxation affects disposable income. Higher income tax reduces disposable income, so households have less money available for consumption.

Disposable Income = Gross Income + Cash Welfare Benefits − Income Taxes

Lower income tax or lower indirect taxes can increase consumption because households have more income available to spend.

Consumer Confidence

Consumer confidence refers to how optimistic households feel about their future income, job security and the general state of the economy.

If consumers are confident, they are more likely to spend. If they expect unemployment or lower income in the future, they may reduce consumption and increase saving.

Interest Rates

Interest rates are the cost of borrowing and the reward for saving. Higher interest rates make saving more attractive and borrowing more expensive.

Therefore, higher interest rates usually reduce consumption, especially spending on expensive consumer durables that are often bought using credit.

Availability of Credit

The availability of credit refers to how easy it is for households to borrow money from banks or other financial institutions.

When credit is easy to obtain, households can borrow more easily and consumption may rise. When credit is difficult to obtain, consumption is likely to fall.

3. Determinants of Investment

Investment is spending by firms on capital goods, such as machinery, equipment, factories and buildings. Investment can increase the productive capacity of the economy.

Interest Rates

Lower interest rates reduce the cost of borrowing, making more investment projects profitable. Higher interest rates increase borrowing costs and may reduce investment.

MEI and Planned Investment

The marginal efficiency of investment, or MEI, shows the expected rate of return from additional investment projects. A project is profitable if its expected rate of return is higher than the interest rate.

Explanation

The diagram shows how firms compare the marginal efficiency of investment (MEI) with the interest rate before deciding how much planned investment to undertake. The blue downward-sloping line is the MEI curve: as firms take on more investment projects, the expected return on the next marginal project normally becomes lower.

If MEI > interest rate The expected return is greater than the borrowing cost, so the project is worth doing.
If MEI < interest rate The expected return is below the borrowing cost, so the project is not worth doing.
Firms invest until MEI = interest rate This intersection determines the quantity of planned investment.

The red and green horizontal lines are interest rates. They do not determine the shape of the MEI curve; instead, each interest rate acts as a horizontal reference line that cuts the MEI curve and identifies the level of planned investment.

At interest rate R1, the MEI curve is cut at I1. When the interest rate falls to R2, more projects have an expected return above the cost of borrowing, so the intersection moves right and planned investment increases to I2.

One-sentence summary for students

The downward-sloping curve shows the relationship between MEI and investment; the interest rate is a horizontal reference line, so when the interest rate changes, the intersection moves and planned investment changes.

Cost of Capital

If the cost of machinery, buildings or equipment increases, fewer investment projects will be profitable. Therefore, investment may fall.

Cost of Capital and the MEI Curve

An increase in the cost of capital, such as more expensive machinery or buildings, reduces the expected net return from investment projects. This is shown by a leftward shift in the MEI curve.

Explanation

The interest rate is held constant at R. When the cost of capital rises, each investment project becomes less profitable, so the MEI curve shifts left from MEI1 to MEI2.

At the same interest rate, the intersection with the MEI curve moves left from I1 to I2. This means planned investment decreases.

Expectations and Business Confidence

If firms are optimistic about future demand and profits, they are more likely to invest. If firms expect a recession, they may delay or cancel investment projects.

Growth of National Income

When national income is growing, firms may expect higher demand for their products. This encourages them to invest in more capital to increase output.

Spare Capacity

If firms have a lot of spare capacity, they can increase output without investing in new capital. If the economy is close to full capacity, firms are more likely to invest.

Changes in Technology

New technology can encourage investment because firms may need new machines or systems to remain efficient and competitive.

Profits and Availability of Finance

Firms often use retained profits to finance investment. If profits are high or finance is easy to obtain, investment is more likely to increase.

Taxes and Subsidies

Lower taxes on company profits can encourage investment. Government subsidies for investment can also reduce firms’ costs and increase investment.

4. Government Spending

Government spending is spending by the state on goods and services, such as education, healthcare, infrastructure, defence and welfare.

The level of government spending is often determined by social and political priorities. For example, a government may spend more to improve public services or support people on low incomes.

Government spending can also be used to influence aggregate demand. During a recession, the government may increase spending to offset falling consumption and investment.

5. Determinants of Exports

Exports are goods and services sold to other countries. Exports are an injection into the circular flow of income because they add demand from abroad.

Relative Prices

If a country’s goods become relatively cheaper than foreign goods, exports may increase. If domestic inflation is higher than inflation in other countries, exports may become less competitive.

Exchange Rate

A fall in the exchange rate makes exports cheaper for foreign buyers. This may increase the volume of exports.

A rise in the exchange rate makes exports more expensive for foreign buyers, which may reduce exports.

Quality and Non-Price Competitiveness

Exports are not only affected by price. Quality, design, reputation, branding and customer service can also affect international demand.

Growth in Export Markets

If other countries are experiencing economic growth, their consumers and firms may buy more foreign goods and services. This can increase exports.

Trade Restrictions

Tariffs, quotas and other trade barriers can reduce exports. If trade restrictions are reduced, exports may increase.

6. Determinants of Imports

Imports are goods and services bought from other countries. Imports are a withdrawal from the circular flow of income because spending goes abroad.

Relative Prices

If domestic goods become more expensive compared with foreign goods, consumers may buy more imports.

Exchange Rate

A fall in the exchange rate makes imports more expensive in domestic currency. This may reduce imports.

A rise in the exchange rate makes imports cheaper in domestic currency. This may increase imports.

Domestic Income and Economic Growth

When the domestic economy grows, consumers and firms spend more. Some of this extra spending will be on imported goods, raw materials and components.

Quality and Design

Consumers may buy imports if foreign products are seen as higher quality, better designed or more reliable.

Trade Restrictions

Tariffs and quotas can reduce imports by making foreign goods more expensive or limiting the amount that can be imported.

7. Determinants of Savings

Savings are income not spent on current consumption. Savings are a withdrawal from the circular flow of income, so higher savings may reduce aggregate demand in the short run.

Income

As income increases, total household saving is likely to increase. However, the proportion of income saved may vary between households and countries.

Savings Ratio = Household Savings ÷ Household Disposable Income × 100

Interest Rates

Higher interest rates make saving more rewarding, so households may save more. However, households are more likely to consider the real interest rate.

Real Interest Rate = Nominal Interest Rate − Inflation Rate

Expectations and Confidence

If households are worried about unemployment or future income, they may increase saving for precautionary reasons.

Age

People may save during their working lives and use their savings after retirement. Therefore, the age structure of the population can affect the savings ratio.

Taxation and Welfare Provision

Higher taxation can reduce households’ ability to save. Generous welfare benefits, pensions, healthcare or education provision may reduce the need for private saving.

Financial System

A developed and trusted financial system can encourage saving because people have safe and convenient ways to deposit and withdraw money.

Social Attitudes

In some societies, saving is encouraged and borrowing is discouraged. In these societies, the savings ratio may be higher.

8. Exam Tip

When explaining a change in aggregate demand, always identify which component changes: consumption, investment, government spending, exports or imports.

Then explain the direction of the impact:

  • If C, I, G or X increases, AD shifts to the right.
  • If C, I, G or X decreases, AD shifts to the left.
  • If M increases, net exports fall, so AD may shift to the left.
  • If M decreases, net exports rise, so AD may shift to the right.

A strong exam answer should include both the chain of reasoning and the final effect on aggregate demand.

Class Activity

Activity 1: Identify the Component of AD

For each situation, decide which component of aggregate demand is affected: C, I, G, X or M.

  1. Households become more confident about future employment.
  2. The government increases spending on public hospitals.
  3. Firms buy more machinery to expand production.
  4. The exchange rate falls, making exports cheaper.
  5. Domestic consumers buy more imported cars.

Activity 2: MPC Calculation

National income increases by $200 million. The marginal propensity to consume is 0.75.

Calculate the increase in consumption.

Change in Consumption = MPC × Change in Income

Answer: 0.75 × $200 million = $150 million

Key Takeaway

Big Idea

Aggregate demand changes when one or more of its components changes. To explain a shift in AD clearly, identify the affected component first, then explain the chain of reasoning.

Example: Lower interest rates reduce the cost of borrowing, so households may spend more and firms may invest more. As a result, consumption and investment increase, causing aggregate demand to shift to the right.
