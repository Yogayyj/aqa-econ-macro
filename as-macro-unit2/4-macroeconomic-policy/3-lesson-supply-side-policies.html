Lesson 3: Supply-side Policies

Policies intended to improve productivity, flexibility and productive capacity—and the private-sector improvements that occur without government action.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish a supply-side policy from a supply-side improvement.
  • Explain how policies can increase potential output and the underlying trend rate of growth.
  • Analyse effects on unemployment, inflation and the current account.
  • Compare education, infrastructure, welfare reform, deregulation, tax changes and industrial policy.

1. Policy is not the same as improvement

Supply-side policy: a government measure intended to improve the productive potential, productivity or flexibility of the economy.
Supply-side improvement: an actual beneficial change in productive capacity or efficiency, shown by a rightward shift of LRAS.

A policy may fail to produce an improvement because of poor targeting, time lags or unintended consequences. Equally, improvements often originate independently in the private sector through invention, innovation, employee training, capital investment and productivity gains.

investment / better skills / innovation→productivity or factor supply rises→unit costs fall and LRAS shifts right→potential output and trend growth rise

2. Major supply-side policies

PolicyTransmission mechanismLimitation or trade-off
Education and trainingRaises human capital, occupational mobility and productivity; can reduce structural unemployment.Expensive, slow and uncertain if training does not match vacancies.
InfrastructureTransport, energy and digital networks reduce firms' costs and connect workers to jobs.Long planning lags, opportunity cost and risk of low-use projects.
Welfare reformChanges the income gain from work and may improve job-search incentives and labour supply.Vacancies may not exist; lower support can increase poverty and inequality.
DeregulationLower entry and compliance barriers may increase competition, enterprise and flexibility.Weaker consumer, worker or environmental protection may create market failure.
Industrial policyTargeted grants, procurement, research support or coordination develops strategic sectors and technology.Government may pick unsuccessful firms; lobbying and opportunity costs can cause government failure.
Tax changesLower marginal income or profit taxes may strengthen work, enterprise and investment incentives.Response may be small, tax revenue may fall, and distribution may become less equal.

3. Tax incentives require careful analysis

Labour market

A lower marginal income-tax rate increases the reward from an additional hour of work. Labour supply may rise, but some workers may choose more leisure after their disposable income increases.

Product and capital markets

Investment allowances or lower corporation tax may increase expected post-tax returns and capital formation. The response depends on confidence, demand and policy certainty.

Exam technique: never jump from “tax is cut” directly to “LRAS rises.” Identify the incentive, the behavioural response, the affected factor of production, and then the capacity effect.

4. Effects on macroeconomic performance

Growth and inflation

A rightward LRAS shift raises potential output and permits AD to grow with less inflation. Lower unit costs can also shift SRAS right and lower the price level.

Unemployment

Training, mobility and matching can lower structural and frictional unemployment. Labour-saving technology may cause transitional job losses even while capacity rises.

Current account

Higher productivity and better quality improve price and non-price competitiveness, potentially increasing exports and reducing import penetration.

Government finances and equity

Higher employment may raise tax receipts, but policies have upfront costs. Market-based reforms may widen inequality unless paired with targeted support.

Supply-side policies cannot guarantee higher actual output when AD is weak. They expand what the economy can produce; sufficient demand is normally required for firms to use the additional capacity.

5. Government policy or private initiative?

Private-sector strengthPossible government roleRisk
Firms respond to profit signals, discover new products and allocate capital.Correct underinvestment where training, infrastructure or research creates external benefits.Government failure from weak information or political incentives.
Competition encourages innovation and cost reduction.Maintain competition, protect property rights and address finance constraints.Excessive deregulation can weaken standards or market power may prevent competition.
Balanced judgement: assess additionality. A subsidy is more effective if it creates training or investment that would not otherwise happen, rather than paying firms for activity already planned.

6. Exam application

Evaluation task: A country has weak productivity, regional structural unemployment and a persistent current-account deficit. Compare infrastructure investment with deregulation. Recommend a policy mix, considering costs, time lags and distribution.
  1. Distinguish a supply-side policy from a supply-side improvement.
  2. Explain how education spending may shift LRAS.
  3. Why might welfare reform fail to reduce unemployment?
  4. Explain how higher productivity may improve the current account.
  5. Give two private-sector causes of supply-side improvement.
