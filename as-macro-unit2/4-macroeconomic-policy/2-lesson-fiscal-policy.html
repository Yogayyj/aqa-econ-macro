Lesson 2: Fiscal Policy

How government spending, taxation and the budget balance influence demand, supply, resource allocation and distribution.

Mind Map Overview

Aggregate demand

  • Contractionary reverses this

Aggregate supply

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish the macroeconomic and microeconomic functions of fiscal policy.
  • Analyse how spending and taxation affect AD and AS.
  • Classify direct, indirect, progressive, proportional and regressive taxes.
  • Explain budget deficits, surpluses, national debt, and cyclical versus structural influences.

1. What is fiscal policy?

Fiscal policy is the government's manipulation of spending, taxation and the budget balance to influence the economy.

Macroeconomic function

Influences total AD, employment, inflation, growth and the budget balance. Governments may deliberately run deficits to support demand or surpluses to restrain it.

Microeconomic function

Changes incentives, income distribution and resource allocation—for example, taxing pollution, subsidising training or funding merit goods.

2. Fiscal policy and aggregate demand

G rises or T falls→injection / disposable income rises→AD rises→multiplier increases real output
StanceMeasuresLikely effect and risk
ExpansionaryHigher government spending, lower taxes or a larger deficit.Closes a negative output gap and reduces cyclical unemployment; may cause inflation, imports or borrowing.
ContractionaryLower government spending, higher taxes or a smaller deficit/larger surplus.Reduces inflationary pressure; may weaken growth and employment.

The multiplier is larger when spare capacity and marginal propensity to consume are high, and leakages into saving, tax and imports are low. Crowding out, implementation lags and low confidence can reduce effectiveness.

3. Aggregate supply, allocation and objectives

Potential output

Infrastructure, education and healthcare can raise productivity and shift LRAS right, although benefits take time.

Incentives

Tax allowances may encourage investment or work. Poorly designed subsidies can create government failure.

Resource allocation

Indirect taxes discourage demerit goods and pollution; subsidies and public provision redirect resources toward merit goods.

Progressive taxes and transfers can make income more equitable. Public investment can support stable growth and jobs, while environmental taxes can internalise external costs. These goals may conflict: a pollution tax can be regressive unless revenue compensates low-income households.

4. Classifying taxes

ClassificationDefinitionExample / caution
DirectLevied directly on income, profits or wealth.Income tax or corporation tax.
IndirectLevied on spending on goods and services.VAT or an excise duty; firms may pass it on in prices.
ProgressiveThe average tax rate rises as income rises.Can reduce post-tax inequality.
ProportionalThe average tax rate remains constant as income rises.A genuine flat percentage of income.
RegressiveThe average tax rate falls as income rises.A uniform consumption tax may take a larger income share from poorer households.
Exam trap: direct does not mean progressive, and indirect does not automatically mean regressive. One classification concerns what is taxed; the other concerns the average-rate pattern across incomes.

5. Budget balance and national debt

Budget balance = government revenue − government expenditure
A negative figure is a budget deficit; a positive figure is a surplus.

National debt is the accumulated stock of outstanding government borrowing. A deficit is an annual flow that usually adds to debt; a surplus can reduce debt. A smaller deficit means debt may still rise, only more slowly.

Cyclical balance

Changes automatically with the economic cycle. In recession, tax revenue falls and welfare spending rises; recovery reverses these automatic stabilisers.

Structural balance

The underlying balance after removing the estimated effect of the cycle. It reflects discretionary policy and longer-lasting spending or revenue commitments.

Evaluation: debt sustainability depends on interest costs, growth, the currency and maturity of debt, investor confidence, and what borrowing finances—not only the debt total.
